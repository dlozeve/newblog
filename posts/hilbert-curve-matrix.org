The [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_curve][Hilbert curve]] is probably the most famous example of a
[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-filling_curve][space-filling curve]]: a 1-dimensional curve which can reach every point
in a 2-dimensional region.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt Animation of the first 7 iterations of the Hilbert curve.
[[../images/Hilbert_plasma_aligned.gif]]

The Hilbert curve is useful for mapping algorithms, because it
provides a nice mapping between 1D and 2D, preserving locality: places
that are close in 2D space will also be close in the 1D coordinates
along the Hilbert curve. A nice illustration of this can be found [[https://datascienceconfidential.github.io//r/graphics/2026/10/26/space-filling-curves.html][in
this blog post]], where they map all battles in Great Britain to display
a simple 2D chart with the date on the \(y\)-axis and the location on
the \(x\)-axis.

I knew about the recursive construction of the Hilbert curve, and the
way to construct it via the [[./lsystems.html][L-system formulation]], but the blog post
also mentions a way to represent the curve as a single matrix,
containing numbers from 0 to \(n^2-1\) along the path of the curve.

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
   0 &  3 &  4 &  5 & 58 & 59 & 60 & 63 \\
   1 &  2 &  7 &  6 & 57 & 56 & 61 & 62 \\
  14 & 13 &  8 &  9 & 54 & 55 & 50 & 49 \\
  15 & 12 & 11 & 10 & 53 & 52 & 51 & 48 \\
  16 & 17 & 30 & 31 & 32 & 33 & 46 & 47 \\
  19 & 18 & 29 & 28 & 35 & 34 & 45 & 44 \\
  20 & 23 & 24 & 27 & 36 & 39 & 40 & 43 \\
  21 & 22 & 25 & 26 & 37 & 38 & 41 & 42 \\
\end{bmatrix}
\]

How can we build this matrix? The Wikipedia page did not explain this
and I couldn't find an explicit construction anywhere, so I tried to
derive it directly.

You start with the base case \(2\times2\) matrix:

\[
H_1=\begin{bmatrix}0&3\\1&2\end{bmatrix},
\]

and from the \( k\times k \) matrix you can build the next one as
follows:

\[
H_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}
H_k^{\top} & R(H_k^{\top})+3\cdot 4^k\\[4pt]
H_k+4^k & H_k+2\cdot 4^k
\end{bmatrix}
\]

where \(R(M)\) is the matrix \(M\) rotated by 180°.

Intuitively, every matrix \(H_k\) starts at the top-left corner, and
ends at the top-right corner. For \(H_{k+1}\), we follow the general
structure of the Hilbert curve (as seen in \(H_1\)):
1. The top-left quadrant needs to start at the top left, but to end at
   the bottom left. A good candidate is therefore \(H_k^\top\).
2. The bottom-left quadrant needs to start at the top-left and end at
   the top-right. This is just \(H_k\).
3. The bottom-right quadrant is the same: \(H_k\) again.
4. The top-right quadrant needs to start at the bottom right, and end
   at the top right. For this we need to transpose \(H_k\) and then
   rotate it 180°.

All of this can be implemented in a few lines of Python with Numpy:
#+begin_src python
import numpy as np

def hilbert(n):
    H = np.array([[0, 3], [1, 2]])
    for k in range(1, n):
        off = 4**k
        TL = H.T
        TR = np.rot90(H.T, 2) + 3 * off
        BL = H + off
        BR = H + 2 * off
        H = np.block([[TL, TR], [BL, BR]])
    return H
#+end_src

I think for most use cases there are better ways than generating the
full matrix (there are explicit formulas to convert \((x,y)\)
coordinates to the index \(d\) on the curve and vice-versa), but I
found this construction interesting as it clearly mirrors the visual
construction of the Hilbert curve.
